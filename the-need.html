The world is undergoing a profound demographic transformation — one that carries immense social, economic, humanitarian, and spiritual implications. For the first time in human history, the number of people aged 60 years and older is projected to surpass the number of children under five. Simultaneously, the largest generation of young people ever to exist is coming of age — over three billion under 25.

These two shifts — an ageing population and an unprecedented youth bulge — are reshaping societies. They are creating new pressures on families, healthcare systems, and social safety nets. And they are happening fastest in the countries least equipped to handle them.

This page is not about abstract statistics. It is about the real, measurable, and urgent crisis that PHINEHAS GENERATION FOUNDATION exists to respond to. Every number here has a face. Every trend has a cost. And every statistic is a reason someone, somewhere, needs us to act.

Section One

An Ageing World That Cannot Be Ignored

The 60+ population is growing faster than any other age group. By 2050, one in five people on Earth will be over 60.

The world is undergoing a profound demographic transformation — one that carries immense social, economic, humanitarian, and spiritual implications. For the first time in human history, the number of people aged 60 years and older is projected to surpass the number of children under five. By 2030, 1.4 billion people will be aged 60 or over. By 2050, that figure will rise to 2.1 billion — more than one-fifth of the entire global population WHO .

What makes this shift even more urgent is where these older people will live. The World Health Organization projects that 80% of older persons will reside in low- and middle-income countries by 2050 WHO — nations where social safety nets, healthcare infrastructure, and elderly care systems are often underdeveloped or entirely absent. In these contexts, ageing is not accompanied by security; it is accompanied by vulnerability.

The fastest-growing segment is the oldest old — those aged 80 years and above. By 2050, 426 million people will belong to this group WHO . They face the highest risk of disability, chronic illness, social isolation, and poverty. Without deliberate intervention, millions will spend their final years without dignity, care, or companionship.

Ageing & Dignity

Where the Burden Falls Heaviest

The overwhelming majority of the world's older population will live in countries least equipped to support them WHO . This means families — often already struggling — must bear the full weight of elder care without institutional support.

In Nigeria alone, the traditional extended family structure that once cared for the elderly is eroding under economic pressure, urbanisation, and migration. Millions of seniors are left without adequate care.

In the United Kingdom, many older people face a different but equally serious crisis: isolation and loneliness. Over one million older people in the UK report feeling lonely most or all of the time. Behind every closed door is a person who gave decades to society and now eats alone every day.

In light of these realities, it is important to consider the eternal destiny of older people, particularly where they will spend eternity after this earthly life. 2 Corinthians 4:18 states, "While we look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen: for the things which are seen are temporal; but the things which are not seen are eternal."

It has been observed that, despite the fear of death experienced by many older people due to the uncertainty surrounding life after death NIH Study , some have made little or no preparation for their eternal destiny. This highlights the importance of spiritual preparation in old age. Consequently, Christian older adults are often encouraged to remain committed to their service to God and to cultivate a strong and personal relationship with Jesus Christ. Such commitment provides them with the assurance and hope of eternal life and of reigning with Christ in eternity.

Section Two

The Next Generation

While the world ages, it is simultaneously experiencing the largest youth population in human history.

While the world ages, it is simultaneously experiencing the largest youth population in human history. There are 1.3 billion adolescents aged 10–19 years alive today — approximately one-sixth of the global population. When expanded to include all young people under 25, the figure exceeds 3 billion — representing roughly 42% of humanity WHO .

The geographic distribution of these young people mirrors the ageing crisis: nearly 90% of adolescents live in low- and middle-income countries WHO . These are young people growing up in environments where access to quality education, mentorship, healthcare, and economic opportunity is severely limited. Without intervention, their potential remains untapped — and the consequences ripple across generations.

The World Health Organization reports that 1 in 7 adolescents experiences a mental health disorder WHO . Depression, anxiety, and behavioural disorders are among the leading causes of illness and disability among young people. Yet, in most low-resource settings, mental health services for adolescents are virtually nonexistent.

The Silence Around Mental Health

In many of the communities we serve — in Nigeria, in the UK, and across the world — mental health remains a taboo subject. Young people are told to "pray about it" or "get over it." Elderly people assume their despair is simply part of ageing. And those who do speak up often find no services waiting for them.

The WHO reports that most adolescent mental health disorders go undetected and untreated — especially in low- and middle-income countries WHO . The result is not just individual suffering — it is lost potential, broken families, and futures that never unfold.

In the older generation, the situation is compounded by isolation. Studies show that 44.2% of older adults experience death anxiety, and over 92% report at least a moderate fear of death NIH Study . Without community, dignity, and purpose, these fears overwhelm.

Dignity & Safety

736 Million Women and Girls

The World Health Organization estimates that 1 in 3 women — approximately 30% globally — experience physical or sexual violence during their lifetime. That translates to 736 million women and girls who have endured violence from an intimate partner or sexual violence from a non-partner WHO .

Violence against women is not merely a personal tragedy. It is a public health crisis of epidemic proportions. It crosses every border, culture, and socioeconomic class — but its impact is most severe where legal protections are weakest and support services are unavailable.

Prevention starts with reshaping attitudes early — teaching boys respect, responsibility, and empathy before violence ever takes root. That is why we invest in youth education as a form of prevention.

A Crisis That Begins in Adolescence

According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime, 296 million people worldwide used drugs in 2021 — a figure that has grown significantly over the past decade UNODC .

Young people are disproportionately affected. Substance abuse often begins in adolescence — fuelled by peer pressure, trauma, lack of guidance, and economic hopelessness. Studies in Nigeria show the mean age of substance abuse initiation is as young as 12.4 years NIH Study .

Without early intervention, addiction derails education, destroys families, and perpetuates cycles of poverty. The most effective prevention is mentorship, self-worth, and community — the very things we invest in.
